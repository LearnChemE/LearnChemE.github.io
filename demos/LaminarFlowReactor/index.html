Directions

Isothermal tubular reactor in laminar flow with a parabolic velocity profile. Reaction A → products, rate = k CAn. Compare with a plug flow reactor (PFR) and a CSTR of the same residence time τ.

Damköhler number (Da) sets how much reaction happens during the average residence time. Try Da = 1, then raise it toward 10 and watch the conversion of the laminar reactor fall behind the PFR.

reaction order changes how strongly the slow-moving wall fluid and the fast-moving centerline fluid respond to the concentration they see.

radial diffusion (Φ) is off by default, so every streamline is its own batch reactor. Increase Φ to let molecules move across streamlines; the laminar reactor approaches plug flow behavior.

Velocity and conversion. Fluid near the wall moves slowly, stays in the reactor longest, and is most converted. Fluid near the axis leaves in about half the average residence time. Radial diffusion (Φ > 0) flattens the profile.

Marker. Drag the dashed line in the reactor, or use the axial position slider, to see the radial concentration profile at that location.

Analysis tabs. “Conversion vs Da” compares the reactors over four decades of Da. “Residence times” shows why laminar flow is different from plug flow: the exit-age distribution E(θ).

Details

Fully developed laminar flow in a tube of radius \(R\) and length \(L\) has the parabolic velocity profile

\[ v(r) = 2\bar v\left[1-\left(\tfrac{r}{R}\right)^{2}\right], \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{\bar v} \]

For steady, isothermal, constant-density flow with radial diffusion and no axial diffusion, the mole balance for A with rate \(-r_A = kC_A^{\,n}\) is

\[ v(r)\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial z} = \frac{D}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial r}\right) - kC_A^{\,n} \]

In dimensionless form, with \(c = C_A/C_{A0}\), \(\rho = r/R\) and \(\zeta = z/L\):

\[ 2(1-\rho^{2})\frac{\partial c}{\partial \zeta} = \Phi\,\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\!\left(\rho\frac{\partial c}{\partial\rho}\right) - \mathrm{Da}\,c^{\,n}, \qquad \mathrm{Da}=k\tau C_{A0}^{\,n-1},\quad \Phi=\frac{D\tau}{R^{2}} \]

The equation is solved numerically (finite volumes in \(\rho\), implicit marching in \(\zeta\)). The outlet conversion is the flow-weighted (mixing-cup) average:

\[ X = 1-\frac{\int_0^1 c\,(1-\rho^2)\,\rho\,d\rho}{\int_0^1 (1-\rho^2)\,\rho\,d\rho} \]

No radial diffusion (Φ = 0). Each streamline behaves as a batch reactor whose reaction time is its residence time, \(t = \tau/[2(1-\rho^2)] \ge \tau/2\). The exit-age distribution is

\[ E(t)=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2t^{3}}\quad (t\ge \tau/2), \qquad X = 1-\int_{\tau/2}^{\infty} c_{\text{batch}}(t)\,E(t)\,dt \]

Without radial diffusion, no fluid leaves before \(\tau/2\), and the tail of slow wall fluid extends far past \(\tau\). Conversion is \(1-\int C_{\text{batch}}(\theta)\,E(\theta)\,d\theta\), where \(\theta = t/\tau\).

Radial diffusion (Φ > 0). There is no closed form for \(E(\theta)\) once molecules can cross streamlines, so the curve shown is built by tracking several thousand tracer particles: each advects along its streamline at \(v(r)/\bar v\) and takes a random radial step every \(d\theta\) with variance \(2\Phi\,d\theta\), reflecting at the wall, until it exits at \(\zeta=1\). As Φ increases, fluid that would have been trapped near the wall mixes toward the centerline, so the distribution narrows and shifts toward the plug-flow spike at \(\theta=1\).

Reference reactors. Plug flow: \(c_{\text{PFR}}\) is the batch solution evaluated at \(t=\tau\). CSTR: \(1-c = \mathrm{Da}\,c^{\,n}\). Very large Φ makes the concentration uniform across the tube, so the laminar reactor approaches the PFR result.

Assumptions: constant density and temperature, fully developed laminar flow (Re below about 2100), no axial dispersion, no reaction volume change.
