Directions

This simulation shows how X-ray diffraction is used to determine the structure of a crystalline material. X-rays have wavelengths similar to the spacing between planes of atoms, so waves reflected from successive planes interfere. They reinforce only at angles that satisfy Bragg's law, nλ = 2d sinθ. The first tab shows the geometry behind Bragg's law. The second tab shows the powder diffraction pattern of a cubic crystal and which reflections its structure allows. The third tab gives the pattern of an unknown cubic element so you can index the peaks and determine the crystal structure and lattice parameter.

Bragg's law. X-rays reflect from two adjacent lattice planes separated by distance d. Move the sliders for the incidence angle θ, the plane spacing d, and the wavelength λ. The ray reflected from the lower plane travels an extra distance 2d sinθ (dark red segments). When that extra distance is a whole number of wavelengths, the two reflected waves are in phase and diffraction is constructive. The plot below the figure shows the diffracted intensity from a stack of N planes; click on it to set θ, or use the buttons to jump to a Bragg angle. Increase N to see the peaks sharpen.

Powder pattern. Choose a cubic crystal structure, the lattice parameter a, and the X-ray source. The simulated powder pattern shows only the reflections allowed by the structure factor, labeled by Miller indices (hkl). Hover over a peak, or a row of the table, to see its values.

Index a pattern. An unknown cubic element is scanned. Use the sin²θ ratios to decide the structure, then pick a trial structure: the table assigns h²+k²+l² values and calculates a from each peak. A correct indexing gives the same a for every peak. Enter your structure and lattice parameter and select "Check". Uncheck "show sin²θ columns" to do the calculations by hand.

Peak intensities

The relative intensity of each peak is calculated as

where p is the multiplicity (number of equivalent planes in the family), the term in parentheses is the Lorentz-polarization factor, and s = sinθ/λ. The decrease of the atomic scattering factor and the Debye-Waller factor are combined into one approximate term, f² exp(−2Bs²) ≈ exp(−5.5s²) with s in Å−1, so the intensities are approximate. Families with the same h²+k²+l² (such as 300 and 221) overlap in a cubic pattern, and their intensities are added. Peaks are Gaussian with full width at half maximum 0.20° + 0.10° tanθ; the Kα2 line is not included.

Indexing a cubic pattern

Divide each sin²θ by the first one. If the ratios multiplied by a small integer (1, 2, or 3) give integers that match an allowed sequence in the table, that sequence identifies the structure. The lattice parameter follows from each peak:

A correct indexing gives the same a from every peak. The measured peak positions in the unknown sample include random errors up to ±0.02°. Simple cubic and BCC patterns cannot be distinguished by peak positions alone if fewer than seven lines are measured, because the BCC sequence 2, 4, 6, … divided by 2 matches the simple cubic sequence 1, 2, 3, … until the missing 7 in simple cubic. A shorter wavelength (Mo Kα) records more lines.
