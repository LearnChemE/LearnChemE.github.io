Directions

A nonideal reactor of total volume V and volumetric flow rate v0 is modeled as a combination of ideal reactors: a plug flow reactor (PFR) and a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR), connected in series (either one first) or in parallel. Part of the reactor volume can be a stagnant dead zone, and part of the feed can bypass the reactor entirely.

The residence time distributions (RTDs) are shown in the plot. The exit age distribution E(θ) is obtained by a tracer experiment with a pulse input. The cumulative residence time distribution F(θ) is the integral of E(θ) and can be measured by a tracer experiment with a step input. Tracer measurements are used to determine how nonideal a reactor is and can be used to better model the reactor to determine conversions expected for given feed conditions. For example, a CSTR might be nonideal because a small fraction of the feed flows almost directly to the outlet (bypass) or because some part of the reactor contents is not well mixed (dead zone).

Select first- or second-order kinetics and set the Damköhler number with the slider. Choose the configuration with the buttons, and use the sliders to set the fraction of the active volume that is PFR, the dead-zone volume fraction, and the fraction of the feed that bypasses. In the parallel configuration, a slider sets the fraction of the reactor flow that goes to the PFR.

The schematic shows the flow network; line widths are proportional to flow rates, the area of the dead-zone square is proportional to its volume, and the shading shows reactant concentration CA/CA0. The lower plot shows the exit age distribution E(θ) or the cumulative residence time distribution F(θ), where θ = t/τ and τ = V/v0. Delta functions in E(θ) are drawn as arrows labeled with their areas. The third plot shows conversion versus Damköhler number for the nonideal reactor, ideal PFR, ideal CSTR, and segregation model.

For first-order kinetics, the segregation model gives the same conversion as the combined-reactor model for any configuration, because conversion depends only on the RTD. For second-order kinetics, the conversion also depends on how early or late the fluid mixes, so different configurations with the same RTD (PFR → CSTR and CSTR → PFR) have different conversions, and the segregation model (latest possible mixing) gives the highest conversion for a given RTD.
