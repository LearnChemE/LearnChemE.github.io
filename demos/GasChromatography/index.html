Directions

  1. Choose a sample mixture from the four cards on the left. Each mixture is a blend of three or four components that are injected into the column together at time zero.
  2. Choose a column. The more polar the column, the longer the bar under the column composition. The four stationary phases range from nonpolar to polar. A column whose polarity matches the sample's polarity retains those compounds more strongly and usually spreads them out more — try switching columns to see how retention times change.
  3. Adjust the carrier gas flow rate. Flow rate sets the linear velocity of the carrier gas through the column. There is a sweet spot: too slow and the peaks broaden from longitudinal diffusion; too fast and they broaden from slow mass transfer between phases. Watch the plate count N in the readout as you sweep the slider to find the optimum.
  4. Adjust the column temperature. A hotter column releases every compound faster (shorter run time) but compresses the differences between compounds, which usually lowers resolution. A cooler column separates more cleanly but takes longer. This is the classic speed-versus-resolution trade-off in GC method development.
  5. Press Run to watch the detector trace draw left to right, like the pen on a strip-chart recorder. The chromatogram also redraws instantly any time you move a slider or change the mixture or column.
  6. Read the peak table below the plot for each compound's retention time, area under the peak, capacity factor \(k\), peak width, and its resolution to the next-eluting peak. A resolution of 1.5 or higher is generally considered fully (baseline) separated.
  7. Goal: for each mixture, try to find a column, flow rate, and temperature that fully resolve every peak in the shortest possible run time. Then click Details to see the equations behind what you're adjusting, or Reset to create a new mixture composition.

Details

This simulation combines four standard relationships from gas–liquid chromatography theory. The numbers are chosen for clear, physically reasonable teaching behavior rather than to match a specific real column and compound set.

Retention time

Each compound spends the dead time \(t_M\) moving through the column with the carrier gas, plus additional time held up by the stationary phase, set by its capacity (retention) factor \(k\):

$$ t_R = t_M\,(1+k) \qquad\qquad k = K\,\frac{V_S}{V_M} $$

\(t_R\) retention time · \(t_M\) dead (hold-up) time · \(k\) capacity factor · \(K\) partition coefficient · \(V_S, V_M\) stationary- and mobile-phase volumes.

Effect of temperature

The partition coefficient follows a van't Hoff-type relationship, so \(k\) falls roughly exponentially as the column heats up. Compounds with a larger effective enthalpy of retention \(\Delta H^{\circ}\) (heavier, or a better polarity match to the stationary phase) are more sensitive to temperature — which is why raising the oven temperature compresses the peaks toward each other rather than shifting them all by the same amount:

$$ \ln k(T) \;=\; \frac{\Delta H^{\circ}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\right) + \ln k_{ref} $$

Column efficiency — the van Deemter equation

Band broadening is described by the height equivalent to a theoretical plate, \(H\). It depends on the carrier-gas linear velocity \(u\) through three competing mechanisms: eddy mixing (\(A\)), longitudinal molecular diffusion (\(B/u\)), and finite mass-transfer rate between phases (\(Cu\)):

$$ H = A + \frac{B}{u} + C u \qquad\qquad u_{opt} = \sqrt{\frac{B}{C}} \qquad\qquad N = \frac{L}{H} $$

The flow-rate slider sets \(u\) from the volumetric flow \(F\) and the column's internal diameter \(d_c\):

$$ u = \frac{F}{\tfrac{\pi}{4}\,d_c^{2}} $$

Each peak is modeled as a Gaussian of width set by the local plate count, giving the peak's standard deviation and baseline width:

$$ \sigma = \frac{t_R}{\sqrt{N}} \qquad\qquad w_b = 4\sigma \qquad\qquad N = 16\left(\frac{t_R}{w_b}\right)^{2} $$

Resolution between neighboring peaks

Resolution combines efficiency (\(N\)), selectivity between the two compounds (\(\alpha\), set by how differently the column retains them), and how strongly the later peak is retained (\(k_2\)):

$$ R_s = \frac{2\,(t_{R,2}-t_{R,1})}{w_{b,1}+w_{b,2}} \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{N}}{4}\left(\frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha}\right)\!\left(\frac{k_2}{1+k_2}\right), \qquad \alpha = \frac{k_2}{k_1} $$

Rule of thumb used for the table's status color: \(R_s \ge 1.5\) baseline resolved, \(1.0 \le R_s < 1.5\) partially resolved, \(R_s < 1.0\) not resolved.

How the controls map to the model

The flow-rate slider changes \(u\), which moves you along the van Deemter curve for the selected column — changing \(N\) and every peak width. The temperature slider changes every \(k\) through the van't Hoff relation above. The column choice sets the van Deemter coefficients \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), the internal diameter \(d_c\), and how strongly the stationary phase's polarity interacts with each compound's polarity — which sets both the elution order and the selectivity \(\alpha\) between compounds. The final trace sums each compound's Gaussian peak:

$$ c(t) \;=\; \sum_{i} \frac{A_i}{\sigma_i\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_{R,i})^{2}}{2\sigma_i^{2}}\right] $$
