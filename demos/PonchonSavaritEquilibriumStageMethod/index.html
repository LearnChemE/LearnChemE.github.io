Directions

Set the column

Use Feed composition, Distillate composition and Bottoms composition to fix what the column has to do (compositions are mole fraction n‑hexane); the sliders keep them a sensible distance apart automatically. Feed condition moves the feed from mostly vapor toward mostly liquid — watch the feed point F slide along the vertical at zF and the feed stage shift with it. There's no reflux slider: the minimum reflux Rmin is solved for directly, and the column runs at R = 1.5×Rmin by default. Use the Reflux multiplier slider to dial that up or down and watch the stage count respond — drag it toward 1.0 and the column pinches.

Read the diagram

The blue curve is h‑x, the saturated‑liquid enthalpy; the red curve is H‑y, the saturated‑vapor enthalpy. Purple dashed segments are tie lines, connecting a liquid and a vapor in equilibrium on the same stage. Green segments are rectifying operating lines; teal segments are stripping operating lines — each is a straight mass–and–energy balance running out to that section's difference point, ΔR or ΔS, marked with a bold dot (along with the feed point F). When a difference point would land far off the visible range, its lines are cut off at the frame with a small arrow rather than blowing out the scale.

Step through the solution

The diagram solves immediately when you move a slider. To walk it stage by stage the way it's drawn by hand, hit Reset then Step → to reveal one tie line and one operating line at a time, or press Play to animate through automatically. The row highlighted in the stage table is the optimal feed stage — found, not assumed, as the point where a line from ΔR through the feed first crosses the liquid curve.

About this simulation

This simulator reproduces a classic chemical engineering teaching example — separating n‑hexane from n‑octane by the Ponchon–Savarit graphical method, with a partial condenser and partial reboiler, saturated‑liquid reflux, and the reflux ratio pinned to 1.5× the computed minimum — as an interactive tool instead of a single static hand‑drawn figure. Move any slider and the whole construction re‑solves: the bubble/dew temperature field, the equilibrium curve, the minimum‑reflux pinch tie line, the optimal feed stage, and the full stage‑by‑stage staircase.

What's real and what's simplified: the vapor–liquid equilibrium and enthalpies come from actual Antoine coefficients, heat capacities, and latent heats for n‑hexane and n‑octane — not an idealized curve fit to the sliders. What's simplified is everything a full process simulator would also model: heat capacities are treated as temperature‑independent, the column runs at a flat 1 bar with no pressure drop, and Raoult's law stands in for a proper activity‑coefficient model (a reasonable approximation for two chemically similar alkanes, less so for a mixture with real non‑idealities). Treat the stage counts and duties as a faithful worked example, not a substitute for a process simulator.

Push the sliders toward the edges of their ranges and you may see the red "not buildable" banner — that's the same kind of infeasibility a real design would hit: compositions specified too close together, or a feed condition that puts the pinch tie line somewhere the numerical search can't resolve cleanly. Ease the sliders back toward the middle of their ranges and the construction returns.

Details

The system

This models a real binary pair, n‑hexane (the more volatile component, A) and n‑octane (B), at a fixed column pressure of 1 bar. Vapor–liquid equilibrium comes from the Antoine equation and Raoult's law: at each liquid composition x, the bubble temperature T(x) solves PAsat(T)x + PBsat(T)(1−x) = P, and the equilibrium vapor composition is y = PAsat(T)x/P. Enthalpies are built from that same temperature field: saturated‑liquid enthalpy is a sensible‑heat mixture at the bubble point, saturated‑vapor enthalpy adds each component's latent heat at the dew point. None of this is fitted to the slider positions — it's the same handful of physical constants (Antoine coefficients, heat capacities, latent heats) regardless of what column you ask it to design.

Partial condenser, partial reboiler

Unlike a design with a total condenser, the vapor leaving the top stage isn't fully condensed. The distillate is drawn off as saturated vapor at xD, which sits on the H‑y curve; the liquid reflux returned to the column is whatever liquid composition is in equilibrium with that vapor, one tie line over. That reflux liquid, not xD itself, is the true starting point for stepping stages down the rectifying section — and the partial condenser counts as one of the theoretical stages, same as the partial reboiler does at the bottom.

Minimum reflux, without guessing

At minimum reflux, the operating line's difference point sits exactly on the extension of a tie line — the classic Ponchon–Savarit pinch condition. This tool finds that tie line directly: the one liquid/vapor equilibrium pair whose connecting line, extended, passes through the feed point F. Extending that same line out to xD gives ΔR,min, and the inverse‑lever‑arm rule on the segment from the reflux liquid to the resulting top vapor gives Rmin = L0/D directly, with no trial and error. The actual reflux ratio is then set as a multiple of that minimum.

Governing relations

PAsat(T)·x + PBsat(T)·(1−x) = P    y = PAsat(T)·x / P Antoine + Raoult's law bubble‑point relation, solved for T at every x
HΔR = HV1 + R·(HV1 − hL0)    Qc/D = HΔR − HV(xD) top difference point and condenser duty, from an envelope around the partial condenser
ΔS on the line through ΔR and F, at x = xB    QR/B = hL(xB) − HΔS bottom difference point and reboiler duty, from overall and reboiler envelopes
Liquid enthalpy

hL(x) = x·Cp,AL·T(x) + (1−x)·Cp,BL·T(x), sensible heat only.

Vapor enthalpy

HV(y) = y·[λA+Cp,AVT(y)] + (1−y)·[λB+Cp,BVT(y)], latent heat plus sensible heat at the dew point.

Feed enthalpy

hF = q·hL(zF) + (1−q)·HV(zF), linear in the feed‑quality slider q.

The graphical procedure — enthalpy curves, tie lines, difference points, straight operating lines stepped off until the bottoms composition is reached — is the genuine Ponchon–Savarit method. Every number on this page comes from that same construction, solved numerically instead of drawn by hand.
