Directions

This simulation shows the pressure–temperature (P–T) phase envelope of a methane/n-heptane mixture of fixed composition, calculated with the Peng–Robinson equation of state. Inside the envelope, vapor and liquid coexist. At temperatures between the critical point and the cricondentherm, lowering the pressure at constant temperature first condenses liquid: this is retrograde condensation. It happens in the pink region, which lies between the upper dew-point curve and the curve of maximum liquid volume.

  • Drag the dashed line (or use the temperature slider) to choose a temperature. Drag the large dot (or use the pressure slider) to change the pressure. The cursor changes shape over the parts you can drag.
  • The dot is colored by phase: blue for single-phase liquid, orange for single-phase vapor, and dark red for vapor + liquid. The bubble-point curve is blue and the dew-point curve is orange.
  • The panel at the right lists the phase, the liquid fraction by volume (the fraction of the cell volume filled by liquid) and by moles, the methane mole fraction in each phase, and the phase densities. The cylinder shows the liquid (blue, bottom) and vapor (pale orange, top) volumes in a closed cell. The small plot shows the liquid volume fraction along the current path.
  • Choose a temperature between the critical point and the cricondentherm and press animate depressurization. Starting from single-phase vapor at high pressure, liquid appears as the pressure falls, passes through a maximum (on the curve of maximum liquid volume), and then vaporizes again at the lower dew point.
  • For comparison, choose a temperature below the critical point: the mixture is liquid at high pressure, reaches its bubble point, and the liquid volume fraction then falls steadily to zero at the dew point.
  • Select constant P to move along an isobar instead; the button then reads animate heating. At a pressure between the critical pressure and the cricondenbar, heating the liquid first vaporizes some of it and then condenses it again (retrograde vaporization, pink region on that setting).
  • The methane mole fraction slider changes the feed composition; the envelope is recalculated when you release it. Check log P axis to see the low-pressure dew curve, quality lines to show curves of constant liquid volume percent (5, 10, 20, 40, 60 and 80 %), and P–x–y diagram to show the phase diagram at the current temperature.

Details

For a mixture, the dew-point curve on a P–T diagram extends to temperatures above the mixture critical temperature, out to the cricondentherm, the highest temperature at which two phases coexist. At a temperature between the critical temperature and the cricondentherm, a vertical (constant-T) line crosses the dew curve twice. In an isothermal depressurization from single-phase vapor, liquid condenses at the upper dew point even though the pressure is decreasing (isothermal retrograde condensation). The liquid volume rises to a maximum and then falls back to zero at the lower dew point. Retrograde condensation occurs only between the upper dew point and the pressure of maximum liquid volume; the pink region is bounded by the upper dew-point curve and the curve of maximum liquid volume, which runs from the critical point to the cricondentherm.

Similarly, when the critical point lies below the cricondenbar (the highest pressure at which two phases coexist), heating a liquid at a pressure between the critical pressure and the cricondenbar crosses the bubble curve twice. Liquid vaporizes, passes through a minimum liquid volume, and then condenses as the temperature continues to rise (retrograde vaporization). The retrograde region for heating is bounded by the bubble curve and the curve of minimum liquid volume along isobars.

The P–x–y diagram at the current temperature explains this behavior. The vapor (dew) curve, y versus P, bends back toward lower methane fractions as it approaches the mixture critical point at the top of the loop. A vertical line at the feed composition z1 can therefore cross the dew curve twice.

Peng–Robinson equation of state:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V}-b} - \frac{a\alpha}{\underline{V}(\underline{V}+b) + b(\underline{V}-b)} \] \[ a_i = 0.45724\,\frac{R^2 T_{c,i}^2}{P_{c,i}}, \qquad b_i = 0.07780\,\frac{R T_{c,i}}{P_{c,i}} \] \[ \alpha_i = \Big[1 + \kappa_i\big(1-\sqrt{T/T_{c,i}}\big)\Big]^2, \qquad \kappa_i = 0.37464 + 1.54226\,\omega_i - 0.26992\,\omega_i^2 \]

Mixing rules (van der Waals one-fluid, with k12 = 0) and fugacity coefficient:

\[ (a\alpha)_{mix} = \sum_i \sum_j x_i x_j \sqrt{a_i \alpha_i \, a_j \alpha_j}\,(1-k_{ij}), \qquad b_{mix} = \sum_i x_i b_i \] \[ \begin{aligned} \ln \hat\phi_i = {} & \frac{b_i}{b_{mix}}(Z-1) - \ln(Z-B) \\ & - \frac{A}{2\sqrt{2}\,B}\left(\frac{2\sum_j x_j\sqrt{a_i\alpha_i\,a_j\alpha_j}(1-k_{ij})}{(a\alpha)_{mix}} - \frac{b_i}{b_{mix}}\right) \ln\!\left(\frac{Z+(1+\sqrt2)B}{Z+(1-\sqrt2)B}\right) \end{aligned} \] \[ A = \frac{(a\alpha)_{mix}P}{R^2T^2}, \qquad B = \frac{b_{mix}P}{RT}, \qquad K_i = \frac{y_i}{x_i} = \frac{\hat\phi_i^{\,L}}{\hat\phi_i^{\,V}} \]

Flash calculation (Rachford–Rice, closed form for a binary): the vapor mole fraction V of the mixture is

\[ V = \frac{-\big[z_1(K_1-1) + z_2(K_2-1)\big]}{(K_1-1)(K_2-1)}, \qquad x_i = \frac{z_i}{1+V(K_i-1)}, \qquad y_i = K_i x_i \]

The K values are updated from the fugacity coefficients until the fugacities of each component are equal in the two phases (successive substitution, finished with Newton's method near the critical point). Every value shown is calculated from the equation of state at the selected temperature and pressure; a dash is shown if the calculation does not converge.

Liquid volume fraction: both phases are at the same T and P, so the molar volume of each phase is ZRT/P and

\[ \text{liquid volume fraction} = \frac{(1-V)\,Z_L}{(1-V)\,Z_L + V Z_V} \] \[ \rho_L = \frac{P\,(x_1 M_1 + x_2 M_2)}{Z_L R T}, \qquad \rho_V = \frac{P\,(y_1 M_1 + y_2 M_2)}{Z_V R T} \]

The liquid is much denser than the vapor, so the liquid volume fraction is much smaller than the liquid mole fraction, except near the critical point, where the two densities approach each other. Quality lines are curves of constant liquid volume fraction; they all meet at the critical point.

Constants: methane Tc = 190.6 K, Pc = 46.0 bar, ω = 0.011, M = 16.04 g/mol; n-heptane Tc = 540.2 K, Pc = 27.4 bar, ω = 0.351, M = 100.20 g/mol.

Symbols

a, b = equation-of-state parameters
A, B = dimensionless equation-of-state parameters
kij = binary interaction parameter
Ki = vapor–liquid equilibrium ratio of component i
Mi = molecular weight of component i (kg/mol)
P = pressure (Pa)
Pc = critical pressure (Pa)
R = gas constant (8.314 J/(mol K))
T = temperature (K)
Tc = critical temperature (K)
V = molar volume (m3/mol)
V = vapor mole fraction of the mixture
xi = mole fraction of component i in the liquid
yi = mole fraction of component i in the vapor
zi = mole fraction of component i in the feed
Z = compressibility factor
α, κ = temperature-dependence terms in the equation of state
ρ = density (kg/m3)
φ̂i = fugacity coefficient of component i
ω = acentric factor
subscripts = 1 = methane, 2 = n-heptane, L = liquid, V = vapor, mix = mixture
