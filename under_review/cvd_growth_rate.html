Chemical Vapor Deposition: Reaction vs. Mass-Transfer Control

This simulation calculates the steady-state growth rate of a silicon film deposited by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) from silane, SiH4(g) → Si(s) + 2H2(g), on a wafer. Silane diffuses across a stagnant boundary layer (mass-transfer coefficient hg) and reacts on the surface (first-order rate constant ks). The two steps act as resistances in series.

The left plot is an Arrhenius plot of the log of the growth rate versus 1000/T. The solid blue curve is the actual growth rate; the dashed curves are the reaction-limited (red) and mass-transfer-limited (green) growth rates. The black dot marks the temperature set by the slider; you can also drag the dot or click on the plot to change the temperature.

The right figure shows the silane concentration profile across the boundary layer, the fluxes, and the fraction of the total resistance due to each step. Use the sliders to change temperature, total pressure, silane mole fraction, boundary-layer thickness, and activation energy. Limiting cases. When (low temperature), the process is reaction-limited, , and the growth rate increases strongly with temperature; the slope of the Arrhenius plot is . When (high temperature), the process is mass-transfer-limited, , and the growth rate depends only weakly on temperature ().

Because and at constant mole fraction, the mass-transfer-limited growth rate does not depend on total pressure, but the reaction-limited rate is proportional to pressure. Lowering the pressure therefore shifts the transition to higher temperature, so LPCVD operates in the reaction-limited regime. In that regime the growth rate depends on surface temperature, which is easy to keep uniform, rather than on local gas flow, so many closely stacked wafers can be coated uniformly. APCVD reactors give higher growth rates but usually operate in the mass-transfer-limited regime, where uniformity depends on reactor flow design.

The regime label uses (reaction-limited) and (mass-transfer-limited). The model assumes a first-order surface reaction, dilute-gas diffusion, and a stagnant-film boundary layer whose thickness is independent of pressure.
